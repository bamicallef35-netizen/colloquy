A Colloquy of Signals

after Gordon Pask / Musicolour, c. 1953; Colloquy of Mobiles, 1968 / an homage in software, not a replica / source / benmicallef

Two callers and three answerers sit in a ring. Each has a drive that rises every tick, a signal class (one of six glyphs), and a per-class habituation. A caller whose drive is high enough emits its signal. An answerer whose class matches, and who is not yet bored of that class, answers: the exchange relieves both drives — a transient satisfaction — and raises both parties' habituation to that class, so the next identical signal meets a higher threshold. Habituation is slowly forgotten; a different signal works at once.

You enter through the same channel as a caller. That is Pask's distinction between a conversation and a stimulus–response, made operational: repeat yourself and the system stops listening.

what Pask built, and what this is not

Musicolour (c. 1953, with Robin McKinnon-Wood) was a light show driven by a live performer whose response thresholds rose on repetition, so the machine went dark until the player tried something new. The Colloquy of Mobiles hung in Cybernetic Serendipity at the ICA, London, in 1968: five mobiles with internal drives, exchanging light and sound. This piece carries those two ideas in software. It reproduces neither machine; a full-scale replica of the Colloquy exists (Pangaro and McLeish, 2018, ZKM), and the update rules here are my own.
